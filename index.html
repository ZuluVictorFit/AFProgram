Logging as

Three blocks of eight weeks

The running keeps its six four-week waves. The lifting runs on its own clock: three eight-week blocks, each containing two running waves. Every block gets a full exercise menu swap and its own tempo emphasis, so nothing repeats for more than eight weeks.

The usual rule is that main lifts never rotate, because the percentages are built on them. That rule is right inside a sixteen week season and it does not survive twenty four weeks. Twenty four weeks on one squat variant stops being a stimulus somewhere around week ten, and it is boring long before that. The rotation is affordable as long as you pay the recalibration cost, and the recalibration cost is one set.

Calibration, and why it is not RPE

Hannah undershoots and you overshoot. That rules out an RPE call, so the number comes out of reps performed instead of a feeling.

  1. Week 1, block I. Work up to a true 3 rep max on each main. Estimated 1RM = 3RM × 1.08, which is the mean of the Epley and Brzycki formulas. Every percentage in the block runs off that number.
  2. Weeks 9 and 17, at the block boundary. Convert the old max to the new variant with the factors below, then run week 1 of the block at the prescribed percentage. On the last set only, rep out to technical failure, capped at prescribed reps plus five.
  3. Read the rep-out. Prescribed reps plus four or more, the estimate was low: add 5 percent for the rest of the block. Prescribed plus zero or one, it was high: subtract 5 percent. In between, it was right.

The Friday hinge is capped at 80 percent and does not follow the block ladder. It is written as speed work, and Saturday's long run is the reason. Weeks 21 and 22 take the squat and press to 87 and 90 percent; the trap bar stays where it is.

Cap the rep-out at 8 total on any deadlift variant. Form degrades before the muscles do, and a max-rep trap bar set in week 1 of a block is a bad trade. If either of you would rather just test a 3RM at each boundary, do that instead. It costs a session and it is more accurate.

Accessory intensity: RIR, not RPE

Reps in reserve, not rate of perceived exertion, and the reason is the same one that shaped the calibration protocol. RPE asks how hard that felt, which is where ego and timidity live. RIR asks how many more you could have done, which is a countable question about a discrete thing. Still subjective, but anchored to something you can argue about afterwards.

For anyone who thinks in RPE the conversion is direct: RPE 10 is 0 RIR, 9 is 1, 8 is 2, 7 is 3, 6 is 4.

Most of what is on this card should not be near failure, and that is deliberate. Concurrent training punishes accumulated fatigue harder than it rewards accessory intensity. The main lift carries the strength stimulus. Everything after it is there to add pattern volume, protect a joint, or hold a position, and none of those jobs need a grinder.

Finding the first load, without guessing

Same reps-performed logic as the main lift, so nobody has to rate anything on day one:

  1. Pick a weight you think is about right and do the prescribed reps.
  2. Could have done 5 or more: add 10 to 15 percent, that set was a warm-up.
  3. Could have done 3 or 4: that is your working weight, write it down.
  4. Could have done 0 or 1: drop 10 percent. You just did a main lift by accident.

Then progress reps before load. When you clear the top of the range on every set at the target RIR, add weight and drop back to the bottom of the range. On a 3 x 8 that means working to 3 x 10 before the dumbbells go up, not adding weight every week and wondering why the shoulder is sore in block II.

The hip block, twice a week

This program is sagittal-dominant by nature: squat, hinge, press, run, all straight ahead. The frontal and transverse planes get nothing, and that is where a runner's hip actually fails. It also converges with Hannah's back. Weak hip abductors let the pelvis drop on the swing-leg side, and a pelvis that drops every stride puts a side-bending load through the lumbar spine a few thousand times a run.

Two days, not three, and the carry lives here now. From week 2 the lift days sit Monday, Tuesday, Thursday, which put the hip block on three days with only one clear day between two of them. That is not frequency, that is a repeat. It comes off the squat day, which is the heaviest lower session of the week and already carries the calf and tibialis work, and stays on the upper day and the hinge day. The carry rotates through the block the same way it used to rotate on the hinge day: front-loaded in block I, suitcase in block II, front rack in block III. It sits in the hip block rather than in its own slot because a loaded carry is frontal-plane hip work being sold as a grip exercise. One leg is holding the pelvis level for every step of it. Posture failure ends the set, not distance.

Copenhagen planks will make you sore in a place you have not been sore before. Start on the short lever, knee on the bench, and do not move to the long lever until three weeks in. The adductors are one of the most commonly strained muscle groups in athletes who change direction, and almost nobody trains them until after the first one.

Numbness with a lumbar source: the revised protocol

Multiple shoe changes and multiple lacing changes have already been tried and none of them moved it. That rules out the two cheapest mechanical causes, and combined with known scoliosis and a sciatic history it points the investigation up the chain. The four week protocol is no longer testing equipment. It is testing position, mechanics, and preparation.

First, the things that mean stop and get seen this week, not in four. Progressive weakness, catching a toe or slapping the foot. Symptoms appearing in both legs. Numbness in the saddle area. Any change in bowel or bladder function. Pain that wakes you at night and does not settle with position. You know these better than I do, but they belong on the card so they are not left to memory.

Week 1: baseline and directional testing

Run to onset as normal. The moment it appears, stop and run this test, because which direction relieves it is the single most useful piece of information available without imaging.

  1. Flexion trial. Sit down, or stand and bend forward with hands on knees, for 60 seconds. Does it clear faster than just standing still would?
  2. Extension trial. On a different run, stand tall and gently extend for 60 seconds instead. Better, worse, or no different?
  3. Which leg. Always the same one, or does it switch? A consistent side is a much stronger signal than an alternating one.
  4. Anything proximal at all. Glute, hamstring, calf, low back. Even mild, even briefly. A symptom with a trail up the leg is a different animal from one that starts and stops at the foot.
  5. Terrain. Sooner uphill or downhill? Uphill biases toward hip flexion and a forward lean. Downhill biases toward extension and adds impact. If the two differ noticeably, that is directional information too.

If flexion relieves it and extension provokes it, that is the pattern that fits foraminal narrowing, and it also explains the distance dependence: running is a few thousand repetitions of lumbar extension and the load accumulates. If flexion provokes it instead, the picture leans discogenic and both the deadlift and the sit-up prescriptions need revisiting. Either way you walk into an appointment with a directional preference rather than a description.

Why her hip flexor work looks different to his

The obvious way to build sit-up capacity is hanging knee raises, banded psoas marches and seated leg-lift holds. For a suspected extension-sensitive lumbar source, all three work against you. The psoas attaches to the lumbar vertebrae. Train it hard in an unsupported position and it pulls the spine into extension and the pelvis into anterior tilt, which is the position most likely to close down a foramen. Zack keeps one hanging knee raise on his squat day because he has no back reason not to. She gets none of it.

Hers is done with the lumbar spine pinned: dead bug with an active posterior tilt, hollow body, loaded dead bug in block III. Same muscle, same demand, spine held out of extension the whole time. She also picks up hip flexor length work he does not need: half-kneeling stretch with a posterior tilt and a glute squeeze, 2 x 30 sec each side.

The plank is no longer available to her. Sit-ups are the required event in initial training, so the safer lane is off the table and the honest version is this: she has to do repeated loaded lumbar flexion against a clock, and her symptom source is suspected lumbar.

Two things make that manageable. First, the corrected chart puts her max at 45 reps, not 54, which is 1.33 seconds a rep instead of 1.11. Second, the week 1 directional test now decides how much sit-up volume is safe. If extension provokes and flexion relieves, flexion is not her enemy and the volume is likely fine. If flexion provokes, sit-up volume is a real problem and that stops being a programming question.

What the lifting is doing about it

  • The squat position, since the bar is the only one available. High bar rather than low bar for a more upright torso and less lumbar shear, and a front squat block in the middle, which is the most upright position on offer. Sled push in block III is the only truly spine-unloaded lower body work left, so watch how the squat sits with the symptom and drop the load ten percent rather than pushing through.
  • The hip block, three times a week. Glute med capacity so the pelvis stops dropping every stride. The most direct thing in the program aimed at the actual mechanism.
  • Anti-rotation instead of rotation. Pallof presses and suitcase carries. With a curve, the goal is resisting the asymmetry, not adding to it.
  • Unilateral work with honest side-to-side comparison. A curve means a strong side and a weak side. Log both. If the split squat gap is more than about two reps at the same load, that gap is worth naming to whoever eventually looks at the back.
  • Reverse hyper, light, in block III. Widely used for lumbar cases and low risk when loaded light. The evidence base is thin and I am offering it as common practice, not as a studied intervention. Drop it if it does not feel good.
The honest framing. Four weeks of positional data does not replace a workup, and with known scoliosis plus reproducible neurological symptoms you are already past the threshold where getting seen is the reasonable move. What the four weeks buys is that the appointment starts from a directional preference, an onset distance, a side, and a log, instead of "my foot goes numb when I run." That is the difference between one visit and three.

Read the ladder and you can see the logic. Rest goes up as the intensity goes up, with one deliberate exception: threshold has the shortest rest in the block despite being harder than marathon pace, because the currency there is time at the pace rather than quality of a single rep.

One override. On the goal pace weeks, pace integrity beats the ratio. If you cannot hold goal pace by rep three, take more rest rather than running slower — the point is rehearsing that exact speed, and a rep run at the wrong pace rehearses the wrong thing.

Strides, since they are the most misread session on the card

  • About 90 percent, not a sprint. Fast and relaxed. If your jaw is tight or your shoulders are climbing, you have gone past the point where this does its job.
  • Build, do not launch. Accelerate over the first four or five seconds, hold it for ten, then ease out rather than slamming the brakes.
  • Walk the recovery, never jog it. Jogging keeps the heart rate up and quietly turns a mechanics session into a metabolic one, which is the opposite of the point. Strides should cost you nothing aerobically.
  • After the easy run, not before. They want warm legs.
  • Eight is a ceiling, not a target. If a rep feels slow or laboured, that was the last one.
  • Any hamstring sensation ends strides for the day. No negotiation. A detrained runner at speed is the classic hamstring presentation, which is also why these sit at 90 percent rather than flat out.

The sit-up problem, stated honestly

A one minute sit-up test is repeated loaded lumbar flexion, and Hannah's symptom source is suspected lumbar. There is no way to program around that entirely, so here is how it is handled.

  • Week 1's directional test now decides two things, not one. If extension provokes and flexion relieves, the foraminal pattern, then flexion is not her enemy and sit-up volume is likely fine. If flexion provokes, the discogenic pattern, then sit-up volume is a genuine problem and the conversation changes from programming to medical. That test just became the most important thing in week 1.
  • Volume gets spread, never stacked. Three short sessions a week beats one long one. Same weekly reps, a third of the peak load on any single day.
  • Anti-extension work stays in, and it is not filler. Dead bug, side plank, weighted plank. A trunk that resists extension well tolerates flexion work better, and it is what carries the running load regardless of what she tests.
  • The monitoring rule. If a sit-up session reliably makes the next day's run worse, that is directional data. Log it, cut the volume that week, and bring it to whoever looks at the back.
  • 45 in 60 seconds is a cadence problem, not a strength one. The limiter is hip flexor endurance, rhythm and breathing. Practise in short blocks at goal rate before ever practising the full minute.

Volume caps I held the running to

Threshold work stays at or under roughly 10 percent of weekly mileage, and interval-pace work at or under 8 percent. Those are Daniels' caps and they are why wave 4 is five thousand-metre repeats rather than six, and wave 5 is five by 800 rather than six. Write a session that looks reasonable on the page and you can be 20 to 25 percent over those caps without noticing. More threshold is not more threshold adaptation. It is more fatigue carried into Saturday.

Zack's bodyweight, sequenced

185 and 13:42 fight each other. Ten pounds is 5.7 percent of bodyweight and running economy scales close to linearly with mass, so ten pounds of dead weight costs roughly 20 to 30 seconds over two miles at target pace. Ten pounds of lean mass previously carried costs materially less. The fix is sequencing. Surplus of 200 to 300 kcal through week 16, then hold at maintenance through block III and the sharpening waves. Protein 160 to 180 grams. Waist checked monthly, hard stop 34.5 inches. If you reach week 16 at 180 rather than 185, take 180.

Where this breaks

Thursday gets raced. Hannah is the higher risk, because her honest easy pace feels insultingly slow. Chest strap for the first two waves.
Zack chases the deadlift. The trap bar is written submaximal and the reason is Saturday. If he works up, the base stalls and the VDOT ladder slips a wave.
The block boundary gets skipped. Weeks 9 and 17 carry a calibration job. Skip it and you spend eight weeks running percentages off a number that belongs to a different exercise.
The hip block gets treated as a cooldown. It is five minutes and it is the most direct thing in the program aimed at Hannah's actual mechanism. It goes before the accessories when the clock is tight, not after.
The alternate ladder becomes the program. Three straight weeks on rung 2 is a referral, not a training adjustment.
A report date lands mid-wave. Do not compress the sharpening. Pull wave 6 forward, run wave 4 for two weeks, skip wave 5. Cut the middle, protect the taper.

Reference material. Dietz and Peterson, Triphasic Training, for the eccentric to isometric to concentric block sequence and the argument for training the underlying quality rather than rehearsing the test. Jamieson, Ultimate MMA Conditioning, for the cardiac output method. NSCA, Essentials of Tactical Strength and Conditioning, for heart rate zone methods, injury patterns in military populations, and the finding that strength and aerobic capacity hold on reduced frequency provided intensity is held. Haff and Triplett, Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning, 4th ed, for chronic aerobic adaptation and block periodization structure. Zatsiorsky, Kraemer and Fry, Science and Practice of Strength Training, for loading zones.

The arithmetic. VDOT values, equivalent race times, training paces and the threshold and interval volume caps come from Daniels' published regressions and guidance. Estimated 1RM from a 3RM uses 1.08, the mean of the Epley and Brzycki formulas. The variant conversion factors are gym rules of thumb rather than research, which is what the rep-out check exists to correct. Standards are the age 30-34 column of the Final USAF PFRA Scoring charts effective 1 March 2026, including the height-by-waist lookup.
